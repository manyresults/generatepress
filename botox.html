What Is Botox?

A Trusted Medicine With Far-Reaching Uses

Botox (botulinum A toxin) is a powerful medicine that temporarily relaxes muscles where it is injected. It is best known for cosmetics, but it has far-reaching medical applications — including muscle spasticity, headaches, and urinary incontinence.

When injected into the bladder, Botox calms the overactive muscle that drives urgency and leaks. It is well tolerated, treats conditions ranging from everyday overactive bladder to severely spastic bladders from neurological disease, and can be repeated indefinitely — there is no limit on how long you use it.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions About Botox

Straight answers about how bladder Botox works, what the procedure is like, and what to expect afterward.

How does Botox treat urinary incontinence?

An overactive bladder squeezes when it shouldn't, creating sudden urges and leaks. Botox relaxes that overactive bladder muscle, reducing the involuntary contractions — so you feel fewer urges and have fewer accidents.

How is Botox administered?

Botox is injected into the muscle of the bladder. First the bladder is flushed with local anesthesia and allowed to thoroughly numb. Then a scope is passed up the urethra (urine channel) into the bladder. A small needle is placed through the scope and several injections are made to spread Botox throughout the muscle of the bladder. Most patients tolerate this procedure well.

How quickly does Botox work and how long does it last?

Many women notice improvement within a few days to two weeks as the medication takes full effect. Relief typically lasts 6–12 months. When symptoms begin to return, the treatment is simply repeated — there is no limit to how long you can use it.

What are the risks of Botox?

Bladder Botox is well tolerated. The most common side effects are temporary — mild burning, a small amount of blood in the urine, or a urinary tract infection. Less commonly, the bladder may not empty completely for a period, which can require the short-term use of a catheter. Dr. Littman will review your individual risks and answer every question before you decide.
